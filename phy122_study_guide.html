Topic 1

Vibrations and Simple Harmonic Motion

A body executes Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) when its acceleration is always directed towards a fixed point (the equilibrium position) and is directly proportional to its displacement from that point.

Defining equation of SHM a = −ω²x

The negative sign shows that the acceleration always opposes the displacement — it is a restoring acceleration pulling the body back to equilibrium.

Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

If a particle starts from equilibrium, its displacement at time t is:

x = A sin(ωt)    or    x = A sin(ωt + φ)
QuantityEquationMaximum value
Displacementx = A sin(ωt)A (amplitude)
Velocityv = Aω cos(ωt) = ω√(A²−x²)Aω (at x = 0)
Accelerationa = −Aω² sin(ωt) = −ω²xAω² (at x = ±A)

Period and Frequency

T = 2π/ω     f = 1/T = ω/2π

Common SHM Systems

Simple pendulum
T = 2π√(l/g) — valid for small angles of swing (θ < ~10°), where l is the string length and g the acceleration due to gravity.
Mass on a spring
T = 2π√(m/k) — where m is the mass and k the spring (force) constant.

Energy in SHM

KE = ½mv² = ½mω²(A²−x²)   |   PE = ½kx² = ½mω²x²   |   Total E = ½mω²A² = ½kA²

Total mechanical energy in SHM is constant — energy is continuously exchanged between kinetic and potential forms, and is greatest (all KE) at the equilibrium position and least (all PE, KE = 0) at maximum displacement.

Damped and Forced Oscillations

  • Free oscillation — a system oscillates at its own natural frequency with no external force and no friction.
  • Damped oscillation — amplitude decreases with time due to resistive forces (friction, air resistance).
  • Forced oscillation — a system is driven by an external periodic force at a frequency that may differ from its natural frequency.
  • Resonance — occurs when the frequency of the external driving force equals the natural (fundamental) frequency of the system, producing a maximum amplitude of vibration.
Key facts to remember
  • SHM is defined by a = −ω²x — acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed towards equilibrium.
  • Velocity is maximum at the equilibrium position (x=0); acceleration is maximum at the extremes (x=±A).
  • Period of a simple pendulum does not depend on the mass of the bob, only on length l and gravity g.
  • Total energy in undamped SHM is conserved and proportional to A².
  • Resonance occurs when driving frequency = natural frequency, producing maximum amplitude.
Topic 2

Waves and Wave Motion

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one point to another through a medium (or, for electromagnetic waves, through vacuum) without the permanent transfer of matter.

Transverse vs Longitudinal Waves

Transverse wave
Particle vibration is perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. Produces crests and troughs. Examples: string waves, water surface waves, light and all electromagnetic waves.
Longitudinal wave
Particle vibration is parallel to the direction of propagation, producing compressions and rarefactions. Examples: sound waves, seismic P-waves, waves from a tuning fork.
WaveClassification
Sound / Speech / EarthquakeLongitudinal
Water (tidal)Both — particles move in vertical & horizontal components
Light / Heat / Radio & TV / X-raysTransverse

Waves are also classified as mechanical (need a material medium — string, water, sound) or electromagnetic (can travel through vacuum — light, radio, X-rays).

Wave Parameters

  • Amplitude (A) — maximum displacement of a particle from equilibrium.
  • Wavelength (λ) — distance between two successive points in phase (e.g. crest to crest).
  • Period (T) — time for one complete oscillation.
  • Frequency (f) — number of oscillations per second, f = 1/T.
Wave equation v = fλ

The Progressive Wave Equation

y = A sin(ωt − kx)

where ω = 2πf is the angular frequency and k = 2π/λ is the wave number (propagation constant). This equation gives the displacement y of a particle at position x and time t as a plane wave travels in the positive x-direction.

General Properties of Waves

All waves — mechanical or electromagnetic — exhibit:

ReflectionRefractionInterference DiffractionPolarization (transverse only)
Note A wave whose motion is predictable and repeatable is called periodic; an unpredictable one is called non-periodic or noise. Waves may further be classified as audible, ultrasonic (above the range of hearing) or infrasonic (below the range of hearing).
Key facts to remember
  • v = fλ applies to every type of wave.
  • Transverse waves vibrate perpendicular to travel direction; longitudinal waves vibrate parallel to it.
  • Only transverse waves can be polarized.
  • Mechanical waves need a medium; electromagnetic waves do not.
Topic 3

Nature, Production and Propagation of Sound

Sound is a form of energy transmitted through a fluid (gas or liquid) or solid medium as a longitudinal wave, producing a sensation that is detected by the ear. Sound waves are classified into shock waves (produced when a medium is strained beyond its elastic limit) and elastic waves (produced when the medium retains its elasticity). Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

Characteristics of Sound (Notes of a Musical Sound)

PropertyDepends on
PitchFrequency of vibration only — high frequency gives a high-pitched note, low frequency gives a low pitch.
LoudnessIntensity of sound reaching the listener; proportional to the square of the amplitude of vibration and surface area. Unit: the phon.
Quality (Timbre)Waveform, frequency and intensity — lets us distinguish a note played on a violin from the same note on a flute or piano; depends on the overtones present.

Speed of Sound

In a gas c = √(γP/ρ)   →   (Laplace correction)   →   c = √(γRT/M)

where γ = Cp/Cv is the ratio of molar heat capacities (γ for air ≈ 1.4), P is pressure, ρ is density, R is the gas constant, T the absolute (Kelvin) temperature and M the molar mass. Since γ, R, M are constant for a given gas, the speed of sound in a gas is independent of pressure at constant temperature, and is proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature: v ∝ √T.

On a stretched string (transverse wave) v = √(T/μ)   where μ = mass per unit length (linear density)
Longitudinal wave in a solid c = √(E/ρ)   where E is Young's modulus and ρ is density
Worked example — temperature dependence If the speed of sound in air at 16°C is 338 m/s, find the speed at 0°C.
Using v ∝ √T (absolute): v₀/v₁₆ = √(273/289) → v₀ = 338×√(273/289) ≈ 329 m/s.

General rule: if the absolute (Kelvin) temperature of an ideal gas is doubled, the speed of sound in it also doubles, since v ∝ √T.

Standing Waves in Strings and Pipes

For a stretched string of length L fixed at both ends, the fundamental mode has L = λ/2, so:

f = v/(2L)

The overtones (harmonics) occur at integer multiples of the fundamental: f₁, 2f₁, 3f₁, ...

Worked example A violin string vibrates with a fundamental frequency of 420 Hz. First overtone = 2×420 = 840 Hz. Second overtone = 3×420 = 1260 Hz.

For a pipe/bottle closed at one end and open at the other (resonating by blowing across the top), the fundamental has L = λ/4:

f = v/(4L)

Beats

When two notes of nearly equal frequency (f₁ > f₂) sound together, a periodic rise and fall in loudness is heard — this is the phenomenon of beats, arising from interference of the two waves.

Beat frequency: f₁ − f₂ = 1/t = N  (N = number of beats per second)
Worked example — identifying the unknown frequency A tuning fork of frequency 312 Hz is sounded with a fork of unknown frequency; 4 beats per second are heard. When plasticine (which lowers frequency) is added to the known fork, the beats decrease. Since decreasing beats after lowering the known fork's frequency means the two frequencies are converging, the unknown fork must originally be higher: f = 312 + 4 = 316 Hz.

Rule of thumb: loading a fork with plasticine lowers its frequency slightly. If the beat rate then rises, the unknown note was below the loaded fork's original frequency; if the beat rate falls, the unknown note was above it.

The Doppler Effect

Discovered by Christian J. Doppler in 1845: a change in frequency of a wave is observed whenever there is relative motion between the source of sound (or light) and the observer. This is why the pitch of an approaching ambulance siren drops suddenly as it passes you.

Let: c = speed of sound, us = speed of the source, uo = speed of the observer, f = true (emitted) frequency, f′ = apparent (heard) frequency.

Case I — Source moving toward stationary observer
f′ = cf / (c − us)   (f′ > f, pitch rises)
Case II — Source moving away from stationary observer
f′ = cf / (c + us)   (f′ < f, pitch falls)
Case III — Source stationary, observer moving toward it
f′ = (c + uo)f / c   (f′ rises)
Case IV — Source stationary, observer moving away
f′ = (c − uo)f / c   (f′ falls)
Case V — Both moving same direction, different speeds
f′ = (c − uo)f / (c − us)
Case VI — General formula (both moving, toward/away)
f′ = (c ± uo)f / (c ∓ us)
Sign convention (Case VI, general formula) If the object (observer) and source move towards each other, uo is positive; if moving apart, uo is negative — and likewise for us in the denominator, applied consistently. When the source and observer move in the same direction with equal velocities (uo = us), no Doppler effect occurs. Wind blowing in the direction of the observer effectively increases the wave speed to (c + uw); wind blowing away from the observer gives (c − uw). When a source moves at an angle α to the line joining source and observer, replace us with uscos α in the formula.
Worked example — a moving vehicle passing a stationary siren A police siren has frequency 800 Hz; speed of sound in air = 344 m/s. Find the frequency heard by drivers of vehicles moving at 14 m/s (i) towards the siren, (ii) away from the siren.

(i) f′ = (c+uo)f/c = (344+14)(800)/344 = 832.56 Hz
(ii) f′ = (c−uo)f/c = (344−14)(800)/344 = 767.44 Hz
Worked example — change in pitch as a source passes an observer A car horn (500 Hz) approaches then passes a stationary observer at 20 m/s (c = 340 m/s).
Approaching: f′ = cf/(c−us) = 340(500)/(340−20) = 531.25 Hz
Receding: f″ = cf/(c+us) = 340(500)/(340+20) = 472.22 Hz
Change in pitch = f″/f′ = 472.22/531.25 = 0.9 (i.e. the apparent frequency drops to 90% of its approaching value as the car passes).

Intensity and the Inverse Square Law

Intensity (power per unit area) I = Power / Area   →   for a spherical wavefront: I = Power / 4πr²

Since intensity depends on power and varies inversely with the square of distance from the source: I ∝ 1/r², so for two points: I₁r₁² = I₂r₂².

Worked example A small loudspeaker radiates 25 W. At r = 5 m, I = 25/(4π×5²) = 0.08 W/m². At r = 10 m (twice as far): I₂ = I₁r₁²/r₂² = 0.08×25/100 = 0.02 W/m².

Sound Intensity Level — Decibels

Number of decibels = 10 log₁₀(P₂/P₁)   or   dB = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀)

The reference intensity (threshold of hearing) is I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m², corresponding to the minimum pressure change the human ear can detect, 20 µPa. The threshold of pain is about 1 W/m² (≈120 dB), corresponding to a pressure of 20 Pa. Normal conversation has an intensity of about 10⁻⁵ W/m² (≈70 dB).

Worked example A radio emits sound of intensity 10⁻⁸ W/m². Intensity level = 10 log₁₀(10⁻⁸/10⁻¹²) = 10 log₁₀(10⁴) = 40 dB.

Reflection, Echoes and Reverberation

  • Sound waves reflect wherever they meet an obstruction or discontinuity in the propagation medium.
  • Echo applications: measuring the velocity of sound (v = 2d/t), medical diagnosis (ultrasound), and prospecting for minerals like hydrocarbons.
  • Reverberation is the persistence of sound in an enclosure after the source has stopped, due to (i) direct sound waves, (ii) echoes from walls/ceilings, and (iii) diffused sound within the enclosure.
Sabine's reverberation time formula t = KV / (αA)

where V = volume of the enclosure, A = total surface area, α = average absorptive power of the surfaces, K = a constant.

Resonance

Resonance occurs when a vibrating system produces its largest amplitude because the frequency of an external driving force matches its fundamental frequency. Types: mechanical (e.g. a diver on a springboard), acoustic (resonance tube), and electromagnetic (radio/transistor receivers).

Key facts to remember
  • Pitch depends only on frequency; loudness depends on intensity/amplitude; quality (timbre) depends on waveform and overtones.
  • Speed of sound in a gas is independent of pressure (at constant T) but proportional to √T.
  • Doppler frequency rises when source and observer approach, falls when they recede.
  • Intensity obeys the inverse square law: I ∝ 1/r².
  • 0 dB corresponds to the threshold of hearing, I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m².
Topic 4

Sources of Sound and Sound Detectors

Sources of Sound

Musical instruments are classified by how they produce vibration:

  • String instruments (violin, guitar, piano) — sound is produced by vibrating stretched strings; pitch depends on the string's tension, length and mass per unit length (v = √(T/μ)).
  • Wind instruments (flute, trumpet, organ pipes) — sound is produced by vibrating air columns inside a tube.
  • Percussion instruments (drums) — sound is produced by vibrating membranes or surfaces.

The Human Speech Organs

Speech is produced when air from the lungs is forced past the vocal cords in the larynx, causing them to vibrate. The pitch of the voice is controlled by the tension and length of the vocal cords, while the mouth, tongue, and nasal cavity shape the resonance to form distinct sounds.

The Loudspeaker

A loudspeaker converts electrical (audio) signals into sound. A coil carrying the varying audio current sits in a magnetic field; the resulting force makes the coil (and the attached paper cone) vibrate, producing sound waves that match the electrical signal.

Sound Detectors

Microphones

A microphone converts sound energy into electrical energy — the reverse process of a loudspeaker.

Carbon granule microphone: sound waves striking a diaphragm compress a chamber of carbon granules, changing their electrical resistance in step with the sound pressure. This resistance variation modulates a current flowing through the granules, producing a varying electrical signal.

The Human Ear

The ear has three main parts:

  • Outer ear — the pinna collects sound and channels it via the ear canal to the eardrum (tympanic membrane), which vibrates.
  • Middle ear — three small bones (ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes) amplify and transmit the vibration to the oval window of the inner ear.
  • Inner ear — the cochlea converts mechanical vibrations into nerve impulses sent to the brain via the auditory nerve.

Auditory Response

The normal human ear responds to frequencies roughly between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz. Sound waves of frequency higher than 20,000 Hz cannot be heard by a human and are called ultrasonics. Frequencies below 20 Hz are called infrasonics. The ear's sensitivity varies with frequency — it is most sensitive around 2,000–5,000 Hz.

Key facts to remember
  • Loudspeakers convert electrical energy to sound; microphones convert sound to electrical energy.
  • The three ossicles of the middle ear are the malleus, incus and stapes.
  • The audible range for humans is approximately 20 Hz – 20,000 Hz.
  • Sound above 20,000 Hz is ultrasonic; below 20 Hz is infrasonic.
Topic 5

Reflection of Light at Plane and Curved Surfaces

Laws of Reflection

  1. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (i = r).

Plane Mirrors

The image of a point object O in a plane mirror is located by extending the reflected rays backwards until they meet at the image point I. Since the rays only appear to come from I (they don't actually pass through it), this image is virtual — it cannot be captured on a screen.

  • The image is the same size as the object, and is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
  • The image is laterally inverted ("perverted") — a left hand appears as a right hand in the mirror.
Worked example A man stands 0.5 m in front of a plane mirror and walks directly away at 0.6 m/s. Find the distance between the man and his image after 5 s.
Distance walked = 0.6×5 = 3 m. Distance from mirror = 3+0.5 = 3.5 m. Distance between man and image = 2×3.5 = 7 m.

Rotating Mirrors

When a mirror rotates through an angle θ, the reflected ray rotates through 2θ — twice the angle of rotation of the mirror.

Curved (Spherical) Mirrors

C = centre of curvature, F = principal focus, P = pole. CP = R = 2f

Rules for ray diagrams

  • A ray parallel to the principal axis is reflected through the principal focus.
  • A ray through the principal focus is reflected parallel to the principal axis.
  • A ray through the centre of curvature strikes the mirror normally and is reflected straight back along the same path.
Mirror equation 1/u + 1/v = 1/f     where f = R/2
Sign convention (i) f is positive for converging (concave) mirrors, negative for diverging (convex) mirrors.
(ii) u is positive for an object in front of the mirror.
(iii) v is negative for virtual images, positive for real images.

Image characteristics — concave mirror

Object positionImage
Beyond centre of curvature CReal, inverted, diminished
At CReal, inverted, same size
Between C and FReal, inverted, magnified
Between F and mirror (pole)Virtual, erect, magnified (used as a shaving mirror)

A convex mirror always produces images that are virtual, erect and diminished, regardless of object position — magnification is always less than unity. This is why convex mirrors are used as wide-view/rear-view mirrors.

Key facts to remember
  • Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
  • Plane mirror images are virtual, same size, laterally inverted.
  • Rotating a mirror by θ turns the reflected ray by 2θ.
  • 1/u + 1/v = 1/f, with f = R/2.
  • Convex mirrors always give virtual, erect, diminished images.
Topic 6

Refraction at Plane and Curved Surfaces

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium into another, caused by a change in the speed of light (and hence wavelength) at constant frequency. If the second medium is optically denser, light bends towards the normal; if less dense, it bends away from the normal.

Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)

  1. The incident ray, refracted ray, and normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant for a given pair of media and a given wavelength.
Snell's law n = sin i / sin r

Refractive Index

Absolute refractive index n = c/v   (ratio of the speed of light in vacuum, c, to its speed v in the medium)

n > 1 for every medium except vacuum, where n = 1.

Optical Invariant

For refraction through several parallel media (e.g. air → water → glass):

n sin θ = constant
Worked example Light passes from air (i₁=60°) into water (n=1.33), then into glass (n=1.6). Find r₂.
1×sin60° = 1.33 sin r₁; then 1.33 sin r₁ = 1.6 sin r₂ → sin r₂ = sin60°/1.6 = 0.5413 → r₂ = 32°46′.

Total Internal Reflection

When light travels from a denser medium to a less dense one, the refracted ray bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence increases, refraction angle increases until it reaches 90° at the critical angle, C. Beyond C, no refracted ray exists — all light is reflected back into the denser medium (total internal reflection).

sin C = 1/n

Applications: right-angled prisms and reflectors (periscopes, binoculars), optical fibres, and mirages.

Thin Lenses

Ray rules for lenses

  • A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through the far focal point.
  • A ray through the centre of the lens is undeviated.
  • A ray through the near focal point emerges parallel to the principal axis.
Thin lens equation 1/u + 1/v = 1/f

where u = object distance, v = image distance, f = focal length (positive for converging/convex lenses, negative for diverging/concave lenses).

Combined focal length of two thin lenses in contact 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂
Power of a lens P = 1/f   (in dioptres, D, when f is in metres)
Worked example — correcting long sight (hypermetropia) A defective eye has a near point 100 cm in front of the eye instead of the normal 25 cm. What lens is needed to see an object at 25 cm clearly?
1/f = 1/u + 1/v = 1/0.25 + 1/(−1) = 4 − 1 = 3 → f = 0.33 m.
Power P = 1/f = +3 dioptres (a converging lens).
Key facts to remember
  • Snell's law: n = sin i/sin r; absolute refractive index n = c/v.
  • Optical invariant n sinθ = constant applies across multiple media.
  • Total internal reflection needs: light going from denser→rarer medium AND angle of incidence > critical angle C, where sinC = 1/n.
  • Lens equation 1/u + 1/v = 1/f is identical in form to the mirror equation.
  • Power of a lens (dioptres) = 1/f(metres); converging lenses correct long sight, diverging lenses correct short sight.
Topic 7

Optical Instruments

The Human Eye

The eye focuses light onto the retina using the cornea and the crystalline lens. Two common defects:

Myopia (short sight)
Far objects appear blurred — the image forms in front of the retina (eyeball too long / lens too strong). Corrected with a diverging (concave) lens.
Hypermetropia (long sight)
Near objects appear blurred — image forms behind the retina (eyeball too short / lens too weak). Corrected with a converging (convex) lens.

The near point (closest distance of distinct vision) shifts further away with age as the eye's power of accommodation decreases — from about 7 cm at age 10 to 200 cm or more by age 60.

The Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)

A single converging lens used with the object placed between the lens and its focal point, producing a magnified, virtual, erect image.

The Compound Microscope

Uses two converging lenses — an objective (short focal length, close to the object) that forms a real, magnified image, which is then further magnified by an eyepiece acting as a simple magnifier.

The Refracting (Astronomical) Telescope

Uses an objective lens of long focal length to form a real image of a distant object, viewed through an eyepiece of short focal length. The final image is magnified but inverted.

The Camera

A converging lens forms a real, inverted, diminished image of the object on a light-sensitive film or sensor.

The Periscope

Uses two mirrors (or right-angled prisms relying on total internal reflection) set at 45° to allow viewing over an obstruction.

Prism Binoculars

Use pairs of right-angled prisms (via total internal reflection) to fold the optical path, making the instrument more compact while erecting the final image.

The Projector

A condensing lens concentrates light onto a slide; an objective lens then projects a large, real, inverted image onto a screen.

Linear magnification, m = v/u = v/f − 1

Using an objective of small focal length compared with v gives high magnification.

Key facts to remember
  • Myopia (short sight) → diverging lens; hypermetropia (long sight) → converging lens.
  • Compound microscopes and astronomical telescopes both use an objective + eyepiece pair of converging lenses.
  • Periscopes and prism binoculars rely on total internal reflection or mirrors at 45°.
  • Projector magnification m = v/f − 1; small objective focal length gives high magnification.
Topic 8

Prisms and Dispersion

A prism is a transparent object with two plane refracting surfaces inclined to each other at the refracting angle, A. Unlike a parallel-sided glass block (which does not deviate a ray), a prism bends the emergent ray towards its base.

Deviation Through a Prism

D = (i + i′) − (r + r′)     and     A = r + r′

where i is the angle of incidence, i′ the angle of emergence, and D is the angle of deviation.

Minimum Deviation

The deviation is smallest when the ray passes symmetrically through the prism (i = i′), giving the minimum deviation Dmin.

Refractive index of the prism material n = sin[½(A + Dmin)] / sin(½A)

This formula is used to determine the refractive index of a prism material experimentally, using optical pins or a spectrometer.

Dispersion

When white light passes through a prism, it splits into a band of colours — Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, Violet (ROYGBIV) — called a spectrum. This is dispersion. Violet light is refracted (and deviated) the most, red the least, because the refractive index of glass is slightly greater for shorter wavelengths (violet) than for longer ones (red). Rainbows form by dispersion of sunlight in water droplets acting as tiny prisms.

Chromatic Aberration

Since a simple lens refracts different colours by different amounts (like a prism), it forms images of slightly different size and position for each colour, causing coloured fringes around images — this defect is called chromatic aberration. It can be reduced by combining a converging and a diverging lens of different materials (an achromatic doublet).

Types of Spectra

TypeDescription
Line spectrumDiscrete bright lines at specific wavelengths — produced by excited atoms of a gas (e.g. hydrogen's visible line spectrum).
Band spectrumGroups of closely spaced lines that appear as bands — typically from molecules.
Continuous spectrumAll wavelengths present, with no gaps — produced by incandescent solids, liquids, or gases at high pressure.
Absorption spectrumDark lines across a continuous spectrum, where a cooler gas absorbs its characteristic wavelengths from light passing through it (e.g. the Sun's Fraunhofer lines, caused by hydrogen and helium in the solar atmosphere).

By Kirchhoff's law, a substance that emits light of a certain wavelength at a given temperature can also absorb light of that same wavelength at that temperature.

Key facts to remember
  • D = (i+i′) − (r+r′); at minimum deviation, i = i′.
  • n = sin[½(A+Dmin)]/sin(½A) — the standard method for finding a prism's refractive index.
  • Violet deviates most, red deviates least in dispersion (ROYGBIV).
  • Four spectrum types: line, band, continuous, and absorption (Fraunhofer lines are solar absorption lines).
Topic 9

Wave Nature of Light

Interference and diffraction demonstrate that light behaves as a wave. Huygens' principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets, and the new wavefront is the envelope of these wavelets — this is used to explain reflection and refraction of waves.

Conditions for Interference

Two sources produce a stable (observable) interference pattern only if they are coherent — same frequency, same wavelength, constant phase difference (and ideally similar amplitude).

Constructive interference: path difference = 0, λ, 2λ, ... (phase difference δ = 0, 2π, 4π,...) → maximum intensity
Destructive interference: path difference = λ/2, 3λ/2, ... (phase difference δ = π, 3π, 5π,...) → minimum/zero intensity

Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Two narrow, closely-spaced slits S₁ and S₂ (separation d), illuminated by a coherent monochromatic source, act as two coherent sources. On a screen at distance D, bright and dark fringes appear.

Position of bright fringes z = mλD/d   (m = 0, 1, 2, ... — the order of interference)
Worked example Find the angle of the 4th bright fringe for two slits 0.3 mm apart, illuminated with light of wavelength 700 nm.
Using d sinθ = mλ: sinθ = mλ/d = 4(700×10⁻⁹)/(0.3×10⁻³) = 9.3×10⁻³ → θ ≈ 0.534°.

Thin Film Interference and Newton's Rings

Interference also occurs in thin parallel films (like a soap bubble or oil slick) and in the thin wedge-shaped air gap between a curved and a flat glass surface, producing a pattern of concentric bright and dark rings known as Newton's rings — used to test the curvature/flatness of lens surfaces.

Diffraction

Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or through apertures, causing deviation from straight-line propagation and producing alternating bright/dark bands even in the geometrical shadow region. Two kinds:

  • Fresnel diffraction — involves spherical wavefronts (source or screen at finite distance).
  • Fraunhofer diffraction — involves plane wavefronts (source and screen effectively at infinity); mathematically simpler to analyse.

Diffraction Grating

A diffraction grating consists of a large number of equally-spaced parallel slits/lines (as many as 10⁴ lines per cm). Light passing through interferes to produce sharp maxima.

Grating equation d sinθ = mλ    where d = 1/N (N = number of lines per unit length), m = 0, 1, 2, ... (order of spectrum)

m = 0 gives the central (undeviated) maximum; m = 1 gives the first-order spectrum, m = 2 the second order, etc. Fringe width is inversely proportional to N (the grating constant).

Key facts to remember
  • Interference requires coherent sources — same frequency, constant phase relationship.
  • Young's double slit: bright fringes at z = mλD/d.
  • Diffraction grating: d sinθ = mλ, with d = 1/N.
  • Huygens' principle explains wave propagation via secondary wavelets on a wavefront.
Topic 10

Polarization

If the vibration of particles in a transverse wave remains parallel to a fixed line in space, the wave is said to be linearly (plane) polarized. For light, the electric field vector E defines the plane of polarization (not the magnetic field, since E is responsible for photographic/detection effects). Ordinary light (e.g. from a tungsten filament) is unpolarized — its E field vibrates in random, changing directions, all perpendicular to the direction of travel.

Important Interference and diffraction can occur with any kind of wave (transverse or longitudinal). Polarization, however, occurs only with transverse waves — so the existence of polarization is used as proof that light is a transverse wave.

There are four main ways to produce polarized light from unpolarized light:

1. Polarization by Absorption

Certain naturally-occurring crystals (dichroic materials, e.g. Polaroid, invented by E. H. Land in 1938) absorb light differently depending on its plane of polarization. Long-chain hydrocarbon molecules in the material absorb strongly when the electric field is parallel to the chain axis, but transmit light polarized perpendicular to it — so unpolarized light emerges plane-polarized.

Malus's law (intensity through successive polarizers) I = I₀ cos²θ
Worked example Unpolarized light of intensity I₀ passes through two Polaroids at 22.5° and 67.5° to a reference axis.
After the first: I₁ = I₀cos²22.5° = 0.92I₀. After the second: I₂ = I₁cos²67.5° = 0.38I₁.

2. Polarization by Scattering

Scattering is the absorption and re-radiation of light by particles (e.g. air molecules). Light scattered at 90° to the original beam direction is (partially) polarized. This scattering by air molecules — which scatter shorter (blue) wavelengths more strongly than longer (red) wavelengths — is also why the sky appears blue.

3. Polarization by Reflection — Brewster's Law

When unpolarized light reflects off a boundary between two transparent media (e.g. air/glass), the reflected light is partially polarized. At one particular angle of incidence — the polarizing angle (Brewster's angle), θp — the reflected and refracted rays are exactly perpendicular to each other, and the reflected ray is completely polarized.

Brewster's law tan θp = n₂/n₁
Worked example Light reflected from an oily floor (n₂ = 1.45, in air n₁ = 1) is fully polarized. Find the angle of incidence.
tanθp = 1.45/1 = 1.45 → θp = 55°24′.

Because reflected glare is partially/fully polarized, polarizing sunglasses (Polaroid lenses) are very effective at cutting out glare from wet roads, water, and other reflective surfaces.

4. Polarization by Birefringence (Double Refraction)

First observed by Bartholinus in 1669, birefringence occurs in calcite, liquid crystals, and other non-cubic crystals due to their anisotropic atomic structure — meaning the speed of light through the material depends on its direction of travel (unlike isotropic materials, where speed is the same in every direction).

A birefringent material splits an incident ray into two: the ordinary ray (O), which obeys the normal laws of refraction, and the extraordinary ray (E), which does not. These two rays are completely plane-polarized at right angles to each other and travel at different speeds through the crystal.

Key facts to remember
  • Polarization is possible only for transverse waves — proof that light is transverse.
  • Four polarization mechanisms: absorption (Polaroid), scattering (blue sky), reflection (Brewster's angle), and birefringence (double refraction, e.g. calcite).
  • Brewster's law: tanθp = n₂/n₁; at this angle, reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular.
  • Malus's law: I = I₀cos²θ for light through a polarizer at angle θ to the incoming polarization axis.
